# Liability memo: should HLA-Verify mirror AWS's customer agreement?

*Research memo drafted by an AI agent for Jason Brelsford / Brelsford Software LLC, 2026-09-17. Not legal advice. See disclaimer at the end.*

**Note on scope (updated 2026-09-17 during the terms merge):** this memo's recommended clauses were implemented in `docs/legal/TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` (the single, merged terms document — an earlier `TERMS.md` draft referenced below has been folded into it and removed). The "decision support only; not a medical device" language discussed throughout this memo is, per the product's current positioning, scoped specifically to the `/v1/compat` endpoint and the `donor_compat` MCP tool — the rest of the Service (nomenclature checking, normalization, match arithmetic) is described as reference-release validation, not decision support of any kind. See `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` Section 3.

## Short answer

Yes, mirror the AWS shape, not the AWS scale. AWS's contract does three things that any B2B API should do: it disclaims warranties, it caps liability at money actually paid, and it makes the customer indemnify AWS for how the customer uses the service. All three are standard, enforceable-between-businesses clauses, not something unique to Amazon's bargaining power. Stripe and Twilio, companies with far less leverage than AWS, use the same shape with the same cap formula (fees paid in the prior 12 months). You should adopt that shape in `docs/legal/TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md`. What is different about HLA-Verify is not the liability clause, it is the extra layer needed because the output sits next to clinical decisions: the "not a medical device / research and evaluation only / does not replace clinical judgement" language (scoped to `donor_compat` per the note above), and a hard line on not accepting PHI. That extra layer is the load-bearing part for this business, more than the liability cap itself.

## What AWS actually does

Source: [AWS Customer Agreement](https://aws.amazon.com/agreement/), sections 8-9 (current as fetched 2026-09-17).

- **Disclaimers (section 8).** Services are provided "AS IS," with all express, implied, and statutory warranties disclaimed, including merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose, non-infringement, and quiet enjoyment. AWS also disclaims that the service will be uninterrupted, error-free, or that data will be secure.
- **Limitation of liability (section 9).** Neither party is liable to the other for indirect, incidental, consequential, or exemplary damages, or for lost profits, even if warned the loss was possible. Aggregate liability for either party is capped at the amount the customer actually paid AWS for the service that gave rise to the claim in the 12 months before the claim. Payment obligations themselves are carved out of the cap (you still owe your bill). Courts read an unstated carve-out into clauses like this regardless of the contract's own words: **gross negligence and wilful misconduct are not limitable in most US states**, so a well-drafted version of this clause (and market practice, see Stripe below) says so explicitly rather than relying on the surrounding law to imply it.
- **Indemnification (section 7).** The customer indemnifies and defends AWS against third-party claims arising from the customer's use of the services, the customer's content, or the customer's breach of the agreement or of law. AWS's indemnification obligations to the customer run the other way and are narrower (intellectual-property infringement by the AWS service itself).
- **Acceptable use / life-critical exclusion.** The AWS Acceptable Use Policy and related product terms state that AWS materials are not intended for use in life-critical or safety-critical systems, such as medical equipment operation, automated transportation, autonomous vehicles, aircraft or air-traffic control, nuclear facilities, or live combat. This is a use restriction, not the liability clause, but it does the same job from the other direction: it tells the customer up front which use cases fall entirely on the customer's judgement.
- **HIPAA.** AWS is not itself a covered entity or automatically a business associate. It supports a HIPAA-regulated workload only if the customer (a) uses one of AWS's ~200+ "HIPAA-eligible" services and (b) executes AWS's Business Associate Addendum (BAA), a self-service document in AWS Artifact. Outside of an executed BAA, AWS's default position is that the customer is solely responsible for whether PHI touches the service and for configuring it correctly. AWS does not scan for PHI or enforce this itself; the obligation is placed on the customer by contract.

## Why this holds up for B2B (and where it would not)

- **Limitation-of-liability caps between sophisticated business parties are broadly enforceable in the US**, including under Minnesota law, as long as the cap is not illusory (e.g., $0) and the clause is conspicuous (the ALL-CAPS convention AWS/Stripe/Twilio all use exists specifically to satisfy conspicuousness requirements in some states' UCC case law). Freedom of contract is the default rule for negotiated commercial agreements.
- **Courts will not enforce a cap or exclusion against gross negligence, wilful misconduct, or fraud.** This is close to universal across US states, either by statute or public-policy common law. Write the carve-out into the clause rather than hoping the deficiency never gets tested: AWS's own clause does this, and Twilio's is explicit that indirect-damage exclusions do not apply to certain breach categories.
- **Indemnification clauses running from customer to vendor for the customer's own use, data, and legal compliance are standard and enforceable**, and are in fact the dominant risk-shifting tool in B2B SaaS (Twilio's indemnification clause is broader than AWS's: it applies even to claims arising from Twilio's own negligence, with no carve-out; that is more aggressive than what is recommended below, and more aggressive than what an unrepresented small counterparty would sign without redline).
- **Consumer-protection law does not apply the same way here.** HLA-Verify sells to labs, registries, LIMS vendors, and platform teams, not to consumers, so the state and federal consumer-protection statutes that void or narrow liability waivers in consumer contracts (Magnuson-Moss, state UDAP statutes, some states' bans on liability waivers in consumer services) are not in play as long as the customer is genuinely a business acting in that capacity. Keep the "eligibility and authority to bind" clause in `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` that requires the signer to confirm they are acting for a business, not as a consumer: that representation is part of what keeps you outside consumer-protection scrutiny, not a formality.
- **Where a cap could still fail:** a cap this low ($100 or fees paid) is more likely to be challenged as unconscionable if forced on a party with no bargaining power in a contract of adhesion presented as take-it-or-leave-it, particularly if the harm is bodily injury or the conduct is intentional. That risk is exactly why the carve-outs (gross negligence, wilful misconduct, fraud, and, separately, no cap at all on IP-infringement indemnities in some market templates) exist: they are the pressure-release valve that keeps a court from throwing out the whole clause. Do not skip them to make the cap look tougher; skipping them is what makes the whole limitation-of-liability section vulnerable.
- **Governing law and venue.** Minnesota law, Hennepin County venue (Brelsford Software LLC's home jurisdiction) is standard practice: pick your own forum, require the customer to submit to it. This is enforceable against most business counterparties; it can be challenged by a counterparty with no connection to Minnesota if litigated aggressively, but as a first line of defense (cheaper to litigate at home, deters a customer from suing at all) it is worth having.

## The special posture of a clinical-adjacent tool

This is the part that matters more than the liability cap, because it changes what kind of claim can be brought against you at all, not just how much you'd pay if one succeeded.

- **FDA: Clinical Decision Support (CDS) software exemption.** The 21st Century Cures Act, Section 3060 (codified at 21 U.S.C. § 360j(o)), excludes certain CDS software from the medical-device definition if it meets four conjunctive criteria (FDA's [current CDS guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/clinical-decision-support-software), finalized 2022 and revised 2026):
  1. Not intended to acquire, process, or analyze a medical image, an IVD signal, or a pattern from a signal-acquisition system;
  2. Intended to display, analyze, or print medical information about a patient or other medical information (not acquire it from a device);
  3. Intended to support or provide recommendations to a healthcare provider about prevention, diagnosis, or treatment, without itself producing a specific preventive, diagnostic, or treatment directive; and
  4. Intended to let the healthcare provider independently review the basis for the recommendation, so the HCP is not intended to rely primarily on it.
  All four must be met; failing any one puts the software back inside the device definition and FDA's regulatory jurisdiction.
  - `/v1/verify` and `/v1/normalize` (nomenclature checking against the pinned release) are, if anything, further from "device" territory than CDS at all: they check whether a *name* is real and current, they do not analyze patient data to recommend a clinical action. `/v1/match` is closer to the CDS boundary because a match count is closer to a "recommendation" a transplant team could act on. Treat `/v1/match` as the higher-risk endpoint for this analysis and make sure the criteria above are true of it in practice: the response gives per-locus verdicts and flags but not a go/no-go transplant recommendation, and it is built for the lab's own review, not for a black-box directive.
  - One real gap: HLA-Verify's direct customers include LIMS vendors and AI platform teams, not only licensed healthcare providers directly. Criterion 3/4 are written around "the healthcare provider." Where your customer is a software vendor rather than the treating clinician, you are one contractual layer removed from the HCP the exemption is written for, which is a reason to require your customer's own downstream terms to preserve the "for HCP independent review, not a clinical directive" framing, not a reason to relax your own language.
  - This exemption is why the "research-and-evaluation, not a medical device, does not replace clinical judgement" line is not marketing copy: it is the operative fact pattern that keeps the FDA's device-approval and post-market regulatory apparatus off the table. If HLA-Verify's docs or a customer's promotional material ever described `/v1/match` as producing a diagnosis, a transplant-suitability call, or an autonomous accept/reject decision, that framing alone could pull the product back inside the device definition regardless of what `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` says. Keep the "supports and does not replace clinical judgement" line consistent everywhere the API is described (site, docs, MCP `about`/`disclaimer` field), and keep it clear that this is the `/v1/match` and `/v1/compat` posture specifically, not a claim about the nomenclature endpoints; see `edge/src/mcp.js` (the `donor_compat` tool description and the `about` tool's `disclaimer` field) and `edge/src/docs.js` (the `/v1/compat` section and the Terms paragraph near the end of `DOCS_HTML`), both of which already carry a version of it.
- **HIPAA.** Brelsford Software LLC is not a HIPAA covered entity (not a health plan, health care clearinghouse, or health care provider transmitting standard transactions) and, on the current design, is not a business associate either, because the service is built specifically not to receive protected health information: request bodies are processed in memory and discarded for both REST and MCP calls (per `edge/src/index.js` header comments and `edge/src/mcp.js`), only aggregate per-key, per-request counts and tier land in Cloudflare Analytics Engine, and only a key label plus the Stripe customer's billing email land in Cloudflare KV (per `edge/src/stripe.js`). None of that is PHI on its own. The operative move is contractual, not just technical: `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` must require the customer not to submit PHI or patient identifiers (de-identified allele strings, typing strings, GL strings, and HLA report text only), because if a customer ignores that and sends identifiable data anyway, the "we never store it" architecture reduces exposure but does not retroactively make you not a business associate for the seconds that data was in memory. State plainly, in both the terms and any sales conversations, that **HLA-Verify does not sign a BAA at the self-serve tiers (Starter, Lab, Scale, or the legacy Pro alias)**, and that a BAA is only even a live conversation under a bespoke enterprise agreement, case by case, if the customer's use case genuinely requires it (most should not, given the "no PHI" design). Do not let a self-serve customer talk you into a side-letter BAA over email; it undermines the standard-terms structure and creates exactly the negotiated-relationship posture that undoes a lot of the value of a clean click-through.

## Recommended clauses (implemented in TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md)

1. Warranty disclaimer: "AS IS," no warranty of accuracy, completeness, merchantability, fitness for a particular purpose, or non-infringement; explicit "no reliance" statement stating the customer is not entitled to rely on outputs as the sole basis for any decision.
2. Limitation of liability: exclude indirect, consequential, incidental, and lost-profit damages for both parties; cap direct-damages liability at the **greater of fees paid in the prior 12 months or USD 100** (Twilio's own template uses the same "or $100 if you paid nothing" floor for free-tier users, worth having so the free-tier disclaimer isn't literally a $0 cap, which is the version most likely to be called illusory). Carve out gross negligence, wilful misconduct, and fraud, because a Minnesota court (like most US courts) will not enforce the cap against those regardless of what the clause says, and because leaving the carve-out out invites a court to strike the whole clause instead of just narrowing it.
3. Customer indemnification: for the customer's use of the service, the customer's own data (including any PHI the customer wrongly submits), and any claim by a patient, transplant recipient, or third party arising from the customer's clinical, transplant, or business decisions. Do not go as far as Twilio's version (indemnification even for the vendor's own negligence); that is aggressive even by market standards and is more likely to draw a redline from any customer with counsel, for a company at your stage that mostly wants signed deals, not a fight.
4. Acceptable use: no PHI or personal identifiers, no scraping the reference tables, no reverse engineering, rate limits, no resale without a licence, and explicitly no use of the output as the sole basis for a clinical, transplant, or patient-care decision.
5. Medical-device / clinical-judgement disclaimer: research-and-evaluation tool, not a medical device, output supports and does not replace clinical judgement, customer solely responsible for clinical, laboratory, and transplant decisions and for its own regulatory compliance (including whether its own use of HLA-Verify keeps its product inside or outside the CDS exemption).
6. HIPAA line: not a covered entity, no PHI accepted, no BAA at self-serve tiers, enterprise-only conversation for anything further.
7. Governing law: Minnesota; venue: Hennepin County; optional arbitration clause noted but not adopted by default (arbitration mainly benefits you if you fear class exposure, which is unlikely from B2B customers; skip it unless counsel disagrees).

## In-product measures (implement alongside the terms, not instead of them)

A liability clause nobody agreed to is worth little. In descending order of how much they matter:

1. **Click-through acceptance at checkout.** Turn on Stripe Payment Links' "Require customers to accept your terms of service" setting on the self-serve Payment Links (currently `STRIPE_STARTER_LINK` and `STRIPE_PRO_LINK` in `edge/wrangler.jsonc`, sold to customers as the Starter and Lab tiers — "Pro" is the link's legacy internal name), pointing at `hlaverify.com/terms`. This is the single highest-value item: it is the actual moment of assent for every self-serve customer and it is a Stripe checkbox, not an engineering change. This is Jason's action item, not an agent's: see `IMPLEMENTATION_NOTES.md`.
2. **Key issuance conditional on acceptance.** Because self-serve keys are issued automatically off the Stripe webhook (`edge/src/stripe.js`), acceptance at checkout (item 1) already gates key issuance as a side effect, so no separate gate is needed if item 1 is on. Confirm this is true rather than assuming it; if the Payment Link setting doesn't block completion without acceptance, add an explicit checkbox to the checkout success flow.
3. **Terms link in the product surfaces.** `api.hlaverify.com/docs`, `/pricing`, the footer of every page, and the MCP server's `about` response should all link the terms. `edge/src/mcp.js` already carries a `disclaimer` field ("Research-and-evaluation tool; not a medical device."); add a `terms_url` alongside it so an agent calling the MCP tool has the same notice a human reading the docs page gets.
4. **Terms/attribution in the API response.** The existing `attribution` field on every JSON response is the natural home for a short reference to the terms (e.g., append "See hlaverify.com/terms" or add a sibling `terms_url` field) so a downstream engineer who never saw the checkout page still gets the notice at the point of use, every time.
5. **Footer disclaimer** on every site page, independent of the terms link: "Research and evaluation software. Not a medical device. Output does not replace clinical judgement." Kept short, so it survives being read past.
6. **No-reliance statement**, stated once in `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md` (Section 10) and once in the API docs page (`edge/src/docs.js`'s Terms paragraph near the end of `DOCS_HTML` already has the medical-device line; extend it to state no-reliance explicitly, not just "not a medical device").

## What belongs in an enterprise order form instead of the standard terms

- Any SLA or uptime commitment (no formal SLA is published today at `/docs` or `/pricing`; keyed customers should ask for one in a signed order form).
- A negotiated liability cap above the standard fees-paid/$100 floor, if a customer's legal team insists and the deal size justifies it.
- Any BAA, only ever here, never in the self-serve terms.
- Data-processing terms beyond the standard DPA template (`docs/legal/DPA.md`), such as region restrictions or a named subprocessor list frozen for the term.
- Termination-for-convenience notice periods different from the standard terms.
- Any exclusivity, most-favored-customer pricing, or co-marketing commitment; none of this belongs in a document a self-serve customer clicks through in five seconds.

## What an attorney must review before any of this goes live

- The enforceability of the specific cap amount and carve-out language under Minnesota law, and whether Minnesota is in fact the best venue choice given where customers are actually located.
- Whether the FDA CDS exemption analysis above is correctly applied to `/v1/match` specifically, given its actual current response shape. This memo is a plain-language read of public FDA guidance, not a regulatory opinion, and the four-criteria test is fact-specific.
- Whether any customer's actual use (especially a LIMS vendor embedding HLA-Verify inside a product that itself touches patient records) creates HIPAA business-associate exposure that the "we don't store data" architecture does not fully address. This is a facts-and-circumstances legal question, not just an engineering one.
- The indemnification clause's mutuality and scope, including whether Brelsford Software LLC should offer any indemnity back to the customer (e.g., for IP infringement by the service itself) and on what terms.
- Whether an arbitration clause or class-action waiver should be added, and if so, whether it is enforceable against B2B customers in the relevant jurisdictions.
- Final sign-off on `TERMS_OF_SERVICE.md`, `PRIVACY.md`, and `DPA.md` before they are published or linked from any live page or Stripe Payment Link.
